01 — Featured spread

Maison Verre, refigured

Identity refresh
Eight-week engagement
Paris · 2026
A case in three weights

A house of glass learns to whisper.

Client
Maison Verre, founded 1894
Role
Creative direction · system design
Scope
Identity · packaging · editorial
Duration
January — March 2026

Maison Verre is a fourth-generation glassware atelier that, until last spring, was running on a wordmark designed in 1972 and a website assembled by a cousin in 2014. They were not in trouble. They were in danger of becoming charming, which is worse.

We began the engagement with three weeks of listening. I spent a week in the studio in Biot, watched the gaffers, watched the way light moved across a half-finished carafe at four in the afternoon, and quietly noted that the brand had been telling the wrong story for a decade. They were not selling craft. They were selling restraint.

The work is to find the shape of an existing voice, not to invent a new one. Maison Verre had been shouting in a register they did not own. — Process note, week two

The new system is built around a single editorial idea: every element should feel like it could have existed for forty years and still surprise you on the page. The wordmark is a custom serif drawn against a 1908 Cassandre poster. The packaging uses two colours and one weight of paper. The website, when it ships in June, will have no photography on the homepage.

The studio reports a 34% lift in direct-to-trade inquiries in the first six weeks. They have also, more usefully, stopped apologising for their prices.

02 — Editor's letter

On choosing slowness over speed

Notes from the desk
Paris, March 2026
800 words

Every studio I admire is, on closer inspection, a studio that has learned to wait. They wait through the first round of approved ideas. They wait through the obligatory second round of fashionable ideas. They wait long enough that the third idea — the one nobody can quite explain, the one the founder almost killed at lunch — finally walks into the room and sits down.

That third idea is almost always the only one worth shipping. The first two are tribute to anxiety. The third is the work.

This is, I admit, a strange thing to advertise on a portfolio site. The honest version of brand work runs at the speed of an old vineyard, and almost nobody is selling that. We are mostly selling speed and certainty, two things the work itself refuses to provide.

I started the studio in 2018 because I had spent four years inside a larger agency learning a particular trick: how to perform certainty I did not have, in a sans-serif, on a Thursday afternoon. The trick was useful, lucrative, and quietly corrosive. By the time I left, every brief had begun to sound like the same brief, and every solution like the same solution, sanded down by enough rounds of feedback to stop scratching anyone's expectations.

Slowness is not a luxury here. It is the working method. It is what permits the second-best idea to quietly outgrow the first one. It is what lets a wordmark stop looking like the brand it was responding to and start looking like the brand it was meant to become.

The studio takes two engagements per quarter. The math is unromantic: any more and the work begins to braid. We charge fixed fees against a written scope, and we always begin with a paid scoping session because nothing reveals a project's actual shape faster than asking a client to pay for the first conversation about it.

If you have read this far, you are probably the kind of person we end up working with. Write us a thoughtful note. We will write you a thoughtful note back, usually within two working days, and almost never with a deck attached.

The deck, if there is one, comes later. The note comes first.

— Editor's letter, Issue №012 Camille Rousseau Paris · 14 March 2026

12 February 2026

On the second-best idea

The first idea is anxiety in costume. The second is consensus dressed up as taste. The third — the one you would not be caught dead showing in the first round — is almost always the work. Wait long enough that you can show it without flinching, and the client will recognise it for you.

Method · Editing
28 February 2026

Three weeks of listening

Every engagement begins with three weeks during which I make almost nothing. I sit in the room. I read the old briefs. I watch which adjective the founder reaches for when they are not performing. The mark, when it eventually arrives, almost draws itself. It is the listening that takes time.

Method · Discovery
06 March 2026

What a fixed fee protects

Hourly billing pays you to be slow and punishes you for being good. Fixed fees against a written scope pay you for the answer, not the labour. They also force the difficult, useful conversation about what the scope actually is, before either side has fallen in love with their own version of it.

Business · Pricing
19 March 2026

The kill list

Every engagement ends with a private document listing the ideas I killed and why. It is not for the client. It is for the next version of me, who will be tempted by the same wrong answer in eight months. The kill list is the only honest archive a studio keeps.

05 — Inside the studio

A small room on Charonne

Studio est. 2018
Four people, two cats
One quiet espresso machine

The studio sits on the second floor of a former passementerie workshop in the 11e, with three windows that face an interior courtyard and one window that opens onto the street. There are no agency posters on the walls. There is one bookshelf, which is alphabetised by client surname rather than designer, because clients are the work and designers are colleagues.

We take on two engagements per quarter, run them slowly, and finish them on time. We collaborate with two writers and one developer, all of whom would have come to the launch dinner anyway. We do not have a new business department, because new business is a thoughtful note written back to a thoughtful note received, and that does not require a department.

09 — Frequently asked

Six questions worth answering

Written, not generated
Reviewed quarterly
Honest to a fault
Question 01

How do you decide which projects to take on?

I look for studios who already have taste — a point of view they can articulate but not yet express visually. The work is to find the shape of that voice, not to invent it. If our first conversation feels more like a fitting than a sales pitch, we are probably a good match. The clearer your sense of what you do not want, the faster we can begin.

Question 02

What is a typical engagement length?

Most identity work runs eight to twelve weeks from kickoff to final files. Editorial systems and ongoing art direction tend to live in three or six-month seasons. I take one or two full engagements per quarter — the constraint is by design, not scarcity theatre. Anything shorter than eight weeks is usually a scoping session that lost its temper.

Question 03

Do you work with in-house teams, or only direct clients?

Both. I am often invited as a guest director for in-house teams that already have strong designers but need a steadier hand for a specific season or campaign. My job in those rooms is to set the frame, defend the harder direction in the first two reviews, then leave the team better than I found them. The best engagements end with the in-house team no longer needing me.

Question 04

How do you handle pricing and proposals?

I quote fixed fees by scope, after a paid scoping session. The scoping session is short, written, and produces a brief we can both sign. If we never get past the scoping session, that is a useful answer too — we have not wasted three months pretending. Hourly billing pays a studio to be slow; fixed fees pay for the answer, which is the only thing a client actually buys.

Question 05

Why this insistence on slowness?

Most brand work suffers from being made too quickly by people performing certainty they do not have. Slowness here is not a luxury; it is a working method. Time is what lets the second-best idea — the one you almost killed at lunch — quietly outgrow the first one. Three weeks of listening shortens the next six weeks of making, every single time.

Question 06

Where can I see the work that is not on this site?

A meaningful portion of my work is under NDA, only lives in print, or simply has not yet earned a place in the public archive. I share extended case PDFs with shortlisted clients during the scoping session. If you write me a thoughtful note about your project, I will write you a thoughtful note back, usually within two working days, almost never with a deck attached.
