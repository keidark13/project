Why we built it this way

Fine dining shouldn't be performance art.

We've spent two careers eating at the world's most decorated restaurants. Most of them forgot what they were for. Ash & Ember is the answer to three frustrations we couldn't shake.

01 — Pretense

Service that performs itself.

Captains who recite the provenance of every olive while the food cools. Sommeliers who correct your pronunciation. A whispered theatre that puts guests on edge instead of at ease. Hospitality is not a script. It's reading the room.

02 — Overworked plates

Twenty-seven components on a single course.

Tweezed dots, foam, gels, leaves picked with surgical gloves. Most of it tastes like effort, not food. When a dish needs a paragraph to explain, the cooking failed. We trust three good ingredients over twenty showy ones.

03 — Exhaustion

Four hours of pacing built around the kitchen.

Tasting menus that test endurance instead of feeding people. We serve nine courses in two hours and twenty minutes — not because we're rushed, but because that's how long you're hungry for. The fire decides the rhythm. We just keep up with it.

Our philosophy

A restaurant of fewer choices.

Every cook we know got into this trade because they were obsessed with a fire pit, a grandmother's stove, a single dish at a roadside stand. Then we trained in kitchens that taught us to apologize for that obsession.

Ash & Ember is the apology, rewritten. One menu. One hearth. One seating.

We cook every course over white oak and apple wood, two hands' length from where you sit. There is no induction burner hidden behind a wall. There is no dish that didn't pass through smoke. If you've eaten at a hundred tasting menus, we promise this one will feel different — quieter, hotter, more honest. That's the whole pitch.

  1. I

    Live fire only

    Every protein, vegetable, sauce, and bread touches embers. No induction, no gas burners, no sous-vide. Wood is delivered weekly from a single Hudson Valley supplier.

  2. II

    Single menu nightly

    Nine courses, one menu, every guest. The menu changes weekly based on what arrives from our farms. No substitutions outside of allergies and dietary restrictions.

  3. III

    Open kitchen, open counter

    The fourteen-seat counter wraps the kitchen. You watch every dish you eat being cooked. We pass plates by hand, not through a service window.

  4. IV

    Real conversation

    No memorized scripts. Our cooks introduce their own courses. We answer questions. We laugh. The night should feel like dinner at a friend's, with better firewood.

A different kind of fine dining

Two ways to spend an expensive evening.

Most fine dining

A four-hour formal exercise.

  • Twenty-plus courses, many forgotten by the morning.
  • Three to four hours at the table, paced for the kitchen.
  • Captains who narrate every plate with rehearsed copy.
  • Wine programs designed to upsell, not to teach.
  • Dietary restrictions treated as inconvenience.
  • A bill that surprises you on the way out.
Ash & Ember

A two-hour twenty conversation with fire.

  • Nine courses, paced for hunger and attention.
  • Two hours twenty minutes from amuse to mignardise.
  • Cooks present their own dishes — short, plain, honest.
  • Pairings chosen for fit, listed with prices upfront.
  • Dietary needs prepped with 72 hours notice. No surcharge.
  • Service included in the menu price. No add-ons at the table.

Tonight's menu

Nine courses. One fire.

Week of May 27 – June 1 Subject to morning's delivery
  1. I.

    Smoked oyster, ember-roasted shallot, sea grass

    Montauk oyster pulled raw, finished in a smoking dish of oak shavings. Charred shallot cured overnight in elderflower vinegar. Foraged sea grass from Long Island Sound.

  2. II.

    Aged duck consommé, charred celery root

    A clear broth of Hudson Valley duck, double-clarified and finished tableside with smoked celery root that has roasted eight hours under the embers. Served in handmade ceramic.

  3. III.

    Sourdough at the hearth, cultured butter, salt

    Bread baked one foot from the fire on a hot stone, torn at the counter. Cultured Vermont butter with smoked Maldon salt. A pause before the next three.

  4. IV.

    Spring lamb tartare, ash-roasted egg yolk, ramp

    Pasture-raised lamb from a farm thirty miles north, hand-chopped to order. Egg yolk salt-cured then buried in oak ash to firm. Ramp leaf from this morning's forage.

  5. V.

    Wild striped bass, kelp butter, charred lemon

    Day-boat striped bass from Montauk, set on the grate skin-side down until the edges crackle. Finished with butter cultured with kelp. Lemon blackened in the embers.

  6. VI.

    Dry-aged squab, juniper, fermented blackberry

    Squab aged twelve days, the breast cooked over the highest heat for ninety seconds per side. Juniper smoke caught under a glass cloche, released at the table. Blackberries fermented six weeks.

  7. VII.

    Hudson Valley pork, fennel pollen, embered onion

    A heritage breed pork chop, salt-aged seven days, finished slowly over apple wood until the fat is glassy. Fennel pollen and a single onion buried whole in ash since the morning.

  8. VIII.

    Smoked rye milk, dark honey, beeswax

    A custard of milk smoked with rye berries, cooked low at the edge of the fire. Buckwheat honey from a Catskills apiary. Beeswax shaved at the last moment for scent, not weight.

  9. IX.

    Bittersweet chocolate, oak ash, sea salt

    Chocolate from a single-origin Dominican producer, melted in a cast-iron pan held over coals. Crushed oak ash and flaked salt finish it. One bite, then the night closes.

The kitchen

Two cooks. One fire.

Marcus Vail

Chef & Co-Owner

Marcus grew up cooking on a wood stove in rural Vermont and has spent the twenty-six years since chasing the same heat in restaurant kitchens that no longer used it. He trained at Le Bernardin and Asador Etxebarri, returning to New York with a single idea: a counter where every plate had been touched by fire.

He opened Ash & Ember in 2009 with no investors and twenty-three tables of silverware borrowed from family. The dining room has not grown since.

Imani Reyes

Co-Chef & Pastry

Imani runs the cold and dessert side of the counter — courses one, three, eight, and nine — and is the reason the menu is allowed to end on a single bite. She trained at Tartine and Noma's fermentation lab before joining Ash & Ember in 2014 as pastry sous, becoming partner in 2019.

What you can count on

No surprises at the counter.

01 — Cancellation

48-hour cancellation policy

Cancel by 6 PM two days before service for a full refund. Inside 48 hours we hold the deposit; we'll re-list your seat first and refund if it's filled.

02 — Diet

Dietary restrictions handled

Tell us by 72 hours before service and we'll build a full nine-course parallel menu. No surcharge. We don't believe in a "vegetarian alternative" tucked into the corner of a meat menu.

03 — Allergens

Allergen list on request

Every dish is mapped against the top fourteen allergens. A printed list of the night's allergens is available before you sit. Our kitchen is not nut-free or shellfish-free, but we manage both daily.

04 — Pricing

Service included

The number on the menu is the number on the bill, before drinks. Service is included. Our team earns a salaried living wage with full health benefits. Additional gratuity is welcomed but never expected.

Frequently asked

The honest answers.

Is there a dress code?

Smart casual or semi-formal. We do not require jackets, but most guests dress for a special occasion. Hats and athletic wear are discouraged at the counter. The fire runs hot — heavy layers are a poor choice.

Can I take photos of the food?

Quick personal photos at your seat are fine. We ask that flash, tripods, ring lights, and extended photography sessions stay outside the dining room so the counter experience remains intimate for the other twelve guests. If you're working — a critic, a media booking — please let us know in your reservation so we can plan.

Do you welcome children?

Guests must be 12 years or older to be seated at the counter. The pacing and length of the menu, the late hour, and the open fire directly in front of every seat make it unsuitable for younger diners. We adore children. This room is not built for them.

How do reservations work?

Reservations open at 10:00 AM Eastern on the first of every month, releasing every seat for the 30 days ahead. Tables hold for two minutes in your cart before being released to the next waitlist guest. A $100 per-person deposit is required to confirm. The deposit applies to your final bill.

Can you accommodate vegetarian, vegan, or kosher guests?

Yes. We prepare full vegetarian and vegan tasting menus nightly with the same nine-course structure and the same pacing. Kosher and halal modifications require 72 hours notice so we can source proteins from our partner producers. We do not charge a premium for any of these menus.

How are allergies handled?

We accommodate all common allergens — tree nuts, peanuts, dairy, eggs, soy, gluten, shellfish, fish, sesame. Note allergies in your reservation at least 72 hours before service and our chef de cuisine will reach out directly to confirm the night's plan. A printed allergen list for the night's menu is available on request at the counter.

Is parking available?

We do not have private parking. Street parking on Fulton Lane is metered until 7 PM and free after. The nearest paid garage is at 211 Bedford Avenue, a four-minute walk. The Bedford Avenue subway (L train) is five minutes on foot. We recommend the subway — the neighborhood holds traffic late.

Is gratuity included?

Service is included in the menu price. Our team is paid a salaried living wage with health benefits, regardless of how the night goes. Additional gratuity is welcomed but never expected, and is shared equally among the entire team — kitchen, floor, and dish.
